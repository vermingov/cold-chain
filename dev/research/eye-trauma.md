# The eye and the orbit hurt: what a bullet, a blade, a fist and fragments do, and where src/sandbox/eyes.ts gets it

Tags as in `blunt-trauma.md` and `faces.md`: **[M]** measured in people, **[C]** clinical or textbook statement, **[D]**
derived here, **[E]** a value the game had to choose. Pages marked *(read)* were fetched and read for this pass
(2026-11-14); the rest are the standard sources cited from what they are known to say, not re-read.

## 1. Anatomy, with numbers

- **Globe.** Axial length about 24 mm (23-24.5 in emmetropic adults), volume about 6.5-7 ml, of which the vitreous
  is about 4 ml (some 80 %), a clear gel (99 % water, collagen and hyaluronan) [C] (Snell & Lemp, *Clinical Anatomy of
  the Eye*, 2nd ed. 1998; Remington, *Clinical Anatomy and Physiology of the Visual System*, 3rd ed. 2012).
- **Cornea.** 11.5-12 mm across horizontally, about 0.52 mm thick at its centre, 0.65-0.7 mm at the edge; avascular
  and transparent; the most densely innervated surface of the body (several hundred times the skin's density of
  free nerve endings: Rozsa & Beuerman, Pain 1982;14:105-120; Müller et al., Exp Eye Res 2003;76:521-542) [M].
- **Sclera.** White, about 1 mm at the back pole, 0.6 mm at the limbus, thinnest (0.3 mm) just behind the four rectus
  muscle insertions, which is where it splits in a blunt rupture [C] (Olsen et al., Am J Ophthalmol 1998;125:237-241,
  scleral thickness in enucleated eyes; Radiopaedia "globe rupture" *(read via search)*: ruptures "most common just
  posterior to the insertions of the rectus muscles where the sclera is thinnest").
- **Uvea.** Iris, ciliary body and choroid: the pigmented (dark brown-black) vascular coat inside the sclera. Where
  the wall is open it bulges or extrudes as a dark knuckle; seeing it is a sign of an open globe [C].
- **Anterior chamber** about 3 mm deep, 0.2-0.25 ml of aqueous; intraocular pressure 10-21 mmHg [C].
- **Lens** 9-10 mm across, 4 mm thick; **retina** 0.1-0.5 mm, the only part that sees; the optic nerve leaves 3 mm
  nasal to the back pole [C].
- **Lids.** The thinnest skin on the body (under 1 mm: about 0.5 mm), over orbicularis oculi and the tarsal plate; a
  rich blood supply (the marginal and peripheral arcades), so lid wounds bleed briskly and heal well; the lax tissue
  under the skin takes blood and fluid readily, which is why a black eye swells shut [C] (Kanski, *Clinical
  Ophthalmology*, 8th ed.; blunt-trauma.md: lax periorbital tissue bruises easily).
- **Orbit.** A four-sided pyramid of about 30 ml, 40-45 mm from rim to apex. The rim is thick and strong (it is
  meant to take the blow); the walls behind it are not: the floor (maxilla) averages 0.37 mm at its back medial part
  and 1.25 mm laterally, the roof of the infraorbital canal "only 0.23 mm" (EyeWiki, *Orbital Floor Fractures* *(read)*);
  the medial wall (the ethmoid's lamina papyracea) is about as thin (0.2-0.4 mm); the roof (the frontal bone's orbital
  plate) under 1 mm, with the frontal lobe of the brain on the far side [C]. The apex is the sphenoid (optic canal,
  superior orbital fissure).

## 2. What each weapon does

Terms: BETT (Kuhn, Morris, Witherspoon et al., Ophthalmology 1996;103:240-243, Birmingham Eye Trauma Terminology):
an **open globe** has a full-thickness wound in its wall: a **rupture** from blunt force (the wall bursts from inside
as pressure rises), a **laceration** from a sharp object: **penetrating** (one way in), **perforating** (in and out),
or an intraocular foreign body. EyeWiki *Ruptured Globe* *(read)* uses the same terms.

### Bullet (handgun, rifle)
- A bullet is far wider than the globe's wall is strong: the globe is opened, usually perforated through and through,
  and bursts from the temporary cavity; the contents (vitreous, lens, uvea, retina) are thrown out and the eye
  collapses, flat and wrinkled, often unrecognisable as an eye; the orbit fills with blood and the lids swell over
  minutes. The bullet goes on through the orbit's thin back walls: through the roof or apex into the brain, or across
  into the nose and the other orbit [C] (DiMaio, *Gunshot Wounds*, 2nd ed. 1999, ch. on the head; the game's
  `gunshot-blood.md`).
- Outcome: no light perception at once; most such eyes are removed. Burstein & Lazzaro, Clin Ophthalmol
  2013;7:2335-2340 (urban ruptured globes): 37.5 % primary enucleation among eyes with no light perception, all three
  from gunshots; Esmaeli et al., Ophthalmology 1995;102:393-400 (176 ruptured globes): light perception or none at
  first, wounds behind the rectus insertions, wounds over 10 mm and missile injury predicted poor vision and loss of
  the eye [M] *(numbers read from the search abstracts)*.

### Shotgun pellets, blast fragments
- Small, many: one or several small entry holes in cornea or sclera, often a second (exit) hole at the back (double
  perforation), a foreign body left inside, hyphaema and vitreous haemorrhage; a small self-sealing hole leaves a
  globe of near-normal shape that is nevertheless blind. Fragment wounds are the commonest serious eye injury of war
  (eye injuries ~10-13 % of combat casualties though the eyes are ~0.1 % of the body's front: Weichel & Colyer, Curr
  Opin Ophthalmol 2008;19:519-525; Thach et al., Ophthalmology 2008;115:377-382) [M]. Military perforating injuries:
  median presenting vision no light perception, most eyes removed (Walter Reed series in the search above) [M].

### Blade (knife)
- A thrust into the eye is a **penetrating laceration**: a straight cut in the cornea or across the limbus into the
  sclera, as long as the blade's width there. Aqueous runs out at once (a clear tear), the anterior chamber goes flat,
  the iris is carried into the wound (a dark brown knuckle in it) and the pupil peaks toward the wound (a teardrop
  pupil); deeper, the lens and vitreous come out; blood in the chamber [C] (EyeWiki *Ruptured Globe* *(read)*: peaked
  or irregular pupil, abnormal chamber depth, extruded intraocular tissue).
- The blade can go on through the thin orbital roof into the frontal lobe (transorbital penetrating brain injury), a
  known cause of death from small stab wounds of the eye [C] (forensic texts: Saukko & Knight, *Knight's Forensic
  Pathology*, 4th ed., sharp injury of the face).
- A slash across the lid splits the lid's thin skin: it gapes and bleeds freely (the arcades); one through the lid
  margin notches it; medially it can cut the tear duct [C].

### Punch, bat (blunt)
- **Periorbital haematoma** (black eye): the lax lid tissue fills with blood and swells, over minutes to hours; red,
  then purple, and closes the eye (bruise.ts already does the colour and the swelling) [C].
- **Subconjunctival haemorrhage**: bright red, flat, sharp-bordered blood under the conjunctiva over the white of the
  eye, painless, appears within minutes; a dense one all round the cornea (360 degrees, raised: haemorrhagic
  chemosis) means a ruptured globe until shown otherwise (EyeWiki *Ruptured Globe* *(read)*) [C].
- **Hyphaema**: shearing tears vessels of the iris root and ciliary body; blood in the front chamber, first as a red
  haze, then settling under gravity into a layer with a flat top (EyeWiki *Hyphema* *(read)*: grade I under a third
  of the chamber, II a third to a half, III over a half, IV total; a total, clotted one dark red-black, the
  "eight-ball"; rebleeding within 5 days in 5-10 %; blunt trauma the commonest cause) [C].
- **Commotio retinae**, traumatic mydriasis (a torn iris sphincter leaves a wide, sluggish pupil), lens dislocation
  [C].
- **Globe rupture** from blunt force: needs far more than a fist (a fist is wider than the orbital rim and the rim
  takes it); a bat, hammer or a fall onto an edge can. The wall splits remote from the blow at its thinnest, behind
  the rectus insertions or at the limbus (contrecoup), with dense 360-degree subconjunctival haemorrhage, hyphaema,
  deformed globe, uveal prolapse (EyeWiki *Ruptured Globe*, Radiopaedia *(read)*) [C].
- **Orbital floor blow-out fracture**: something wider than the rim (a fist, a ball) drives the globe back; the
  pressure in the orbit blows out the floor at its thinnest (hydraulic theory, Smith & Regan 1957) or the rim's
  buckling cracks it (Fujino 1974; Waterhouse et al., Br J Oral Maxillofac Surg 1999;37:465-470); EyeWiki *(read)*:
  both are valid by cadaver work. Signs: the eye sinks (enophthalmos) and sits low, though "more commonly develops days
  to weeks after injury as swelling subsides"; double vision on looking up or down with limited movement
  (entrapment of the inferior rectus); numb cheek and upper lip (V2); entrapment can bring "nausea and/or
  bradycardia" with eye movement (the oculocardiac reflex) *(EyeWiki, read)* [C].

## 3. Vision, pain and the reflexes

- **Vision.** Open globe from a bullet or a large wound: no light perception at once (the eye is blind) [M, above]. A
  hyphaema covering the pupil drops vision to hand movements; a vitreous haemorrhage to a red blur [C]. One eye lost:
  no depth from binocular disparity, a field lost on that side [C].
- **Pain.** The cornea's density of nociceptors makes anything touching it intensely painful; a ruptured or
  penetrated eye is excruciating, often with nausea and vomiting (vagal) [C]. A blow-out fracture hurts on eye
  movement [C].
- **Reflexes.** The corneal (blink) reflex: trigeminal in, facial out, both lids close, latency about 40-50 ms for
  its early component (Kugelberg 1952; Aramideh & Ongerboer de Visser, Muscle Nerve 2002;26:14-22) [M]. With a hurt
  eye the lids clamp shut and stay shut (reflex blepharospasm), tears run (reflex lacrimation), light hurts
  (photophobia), and the hand goes to the eye and covers it [C]. A cry or scream is the voice's part (faces.md).
  Pressure on or traction of the globe slows the heart (oculocardiac reflex, Aschner 1908; a drop of over 20 % is
  the usual definition) [C].

## 4. Bleeding

- The eye itself holds little blood: the uvea is vascular but small; a ruptured globe weeps a few millilitres of
  blood mixed with vitreous and aqueous over the first minutes (bloody tears) [C/E]. The front chamber holds only
  0.2-0.25 ml, so a total hyphaema is a quarter of a millilitre [D].
- The lids and the face bleed far more than their size suggests (wounds.ts already gives the face a scalp-like
  rate) [C]. Behind the eye a retrobulbar haemorrhage presses the eye forward (proptosis) and can blind it within
  about 90-120 minutes (orbital compartment syndrome; lateral canthotomy is the emergency release) [C].
- Values used: a ruptured globe weeps about 0.5 ml/min of blood and fluid falling to nothing over a few minutes
  (clots fast) [E]; a lid laceration bleeds at the face's rate.

## 5. Time course used by the game (eyes.ts)

| | at once (0.2 s) | seconds (5 s) | half a minute+ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bullet | hole, globe bursting, contents out, collapsing | flat, wrinkled globe, blood in the socket and running from the lower lid, total hyphaema, dense red over the white | still; lids clamp shut, swelling over minutes |
| Pellet/fragment | small hole(s), little else | hyphaema forming, red spreading from the holes, pupil distorted | eye soft, partly collapsed if the hole is large |
| Knife | straight cut, aqueous out (clear), chamber flat | iris knuckle in the wound, blood in the chamber, red over the white | lids shut, blood and fluid down the cheek |
| Punch | nothing to see but the skin's redness (bruise.ts) | subconjunctival red beginning | red spreads over minutes, hyphaema (some blows) settles into a layer, lids swell (bruise.ts) |

What is not modelled: rebleeding of a hyphaema at days 2-5, sympathetic ophthalmia, infection, the slow enophthalmos
of a blow-out after the swelling goes (days), double vision (nothing in the game sees through their eyes).
